# 0017. Retail price endings cluster on a few digits

Status: Supported
Date: 2026-11-04

## Claim

This record supersedes [claim 0011, Retail price endings cluster on a
few digits](0011-retail-price-endings-cluster-on-a-few-digits.md) after
the pass of 2026-11-04 read whole the five queued works that report a
distribution. The rightmost digit of a retail price is far from uniform.
In every sample read, three digits carry most of the endings and the
remaining digits share a few percent, and which digit leads is a
property of the market and of the digit at which its prices end. The
digit 9 leads in American scanner, Internet and newspaper prices, in New
Zealand, Australian and Norwegian samples and in the low-context
countries of a ten-country web survey, with 5 and 0 next; 8 leads in
Chinese advertisements by their rightmost nonzero digit; and 0 leads in
that survey's seven high-context countries, with 9, 8 or 5 next. It must
convince a maintainer who models the amounts a ledger rounds as
uniformly spread over their last digits.

## Evidence

Read evidence, quoted from the works the passes of 2026-09-30 and
2026-11-04 read in full, with no pin, since no tree produced it. The
three works of 2026-09-30 are quoted as the superseded claim quoted
them.

From the scanner data of one supermarket chain and the daily prices of 474
Internet products [levy2011]: "In Figure 1, we report the frequency
distribution of the last digit of the prices in Dominick's data. If a
digit's appearance as a price-ending were random, then we should have seen
10 percent of the prices ending with each digit. As the figure indicates,
however, about 69 percent of the prices ended with a "9." The next most
popular ending was "5," accounting for only 12 percent of all price
endings. Only a small proportion of the prices ends with other digits."
and, of the Internet data, ""9" was the most popular terminal digit (33.4
percent), followed by "0" (24.1 percent), and "5" (17.4 percent)."

From 1,188 advertised prices in Palmerston North in 1995 [holdershaw1997]:
"In total, 87% of prices were defined as odd prices. Approximately 60% of
prices ended in the digit 9, with a further 30% of prices ending in the
digit 5. Thus, approximately 90% of prices ended in either "9" or "5".
Three digits (0, 5, 9) accounted for nearly 97% of price endings, with the
remaining seven digits accounting for only slightly over 3%." Its Table 1
gives the digits 0 through 9 as 7.5, 0.26, 0.26, 0.76, 0.26, 28.6, 0.26,
0.4, 1.0 and 60.7 percent.

From 499 prices advertised in Shanghai, Hong Kong and Taiwan in 1996
[simmons2003]: "The distribution of rightmost salient ending digits differs
considerably from chance (X2 [9] = 576.97; p < .001)." and "The occurrence
of three digits (5, 8, and 9) was greater than the 10% chance expectation."
Its Figure 1 gives the digits 0 through 9 as 9.2, 1.6, 7.0, 4.8, 1.4, 14.7,
6.2, 3.4, 39.9 and 11.8 percent.

From 1,415 prices in the advertisements of 43 American Sunday newspapers
of 1990-09-09, one price per advertisement, p. 196 [schindler1997]: "As
can be seen in Figure 1, the rightmost digits of advertised selling
prices were not evenly distributed among the 10 possibilities (χ²(9) =
1662.4, p < .001). Three digits, 0, 5, and 9, were greatly
overrepresented; 27.2 percent ended in the digit 0, 18.5 percent ended
in the digit 5, and 30.7 percent of the prices ended in the digit 9.
Thus, more than three-quarters of the advertised selling prices ended in
one of these three digits. The digits 1, 2, 3, 4, and 6 were
particularly underrepresented in the distribution, all five digits
together accounting for only 7.6 percent of the selling price endings."
Its Figure 1, p. 197, gives the digits 0 through 9 as 27.2, 0.9, 1.7,
0.8, 2.2, 18.5, 2.0, 6.6, 9.4 and 30.7 percent, and its Table 2 on the
same page counts them as 385, 13, 24, 11, 31, 262, 28, 93, 133 and 435
prices; pp. 197 to 198: "After an increase from two- to three-digit
prices, the percentage of 9 endings decreases monotonically as price
length increases beyond three digits (χ²(4) = 87.9, p < .001)." and
"This increase in the use of 0 endings is so marked that almost all
prices longer than five digits end in the digit 0."

From the scanner panels of tuna and yogurt in three stores, prices under
a dollar, p. 61 [stiving1997]: "Of the 24,770 prices in the data set
(one for each brand purchased and not purchased for a given purchase
occasion), 50.5 percent ended in the digit 9 and none ended in 0,
leaving 12,260 prices ending in one of the other digits (1-8)." and, of
the yogurt data, "Of the 2,464 observed prices, 36.1 percent end in 9
and 10.9 percent end in 0, leaving 1,306 prices that end with digits in
the range of 1-8." Of the surveys before it, p. 57: "these same price
ending surveys found that the digits 0, 5, and 9 were used in actual
prices over 74 percent of the time and up to 99 percent of the time,
depending on the survey."

From the weekly store-level scanner data of one grocery chain, ten
categories across 83 stores and 399 weeks of 1989 to 1994, p. 123
[mace2012]: "Fig. 1 exhibits the distribution of price endings by digit.
Consistent with prior research (Schindler 2006), the distribution
confirms that the nine-ending pricing practice is intense (on average,
53.6% of prices end with the digit 9). The digit 5 is the second most
common right-hand digit, and then the other digits occur almost equally.
This latter point also pertains to the digit 0, which prior research
often presents as an alternative to nine-endings". Its Figure 1 on the
same page gives the digits 0 through 9 as 4.9, 2.8, 3.5, 4.9, 3.5, 16.8,
3.3, 4.7, 2.1 and 53.6 percent, and its Table 5 the share of 9 by
category, from 43.6 to 67.0 percent.

From the weekly prices of about 3,500 packaged goods in American
supermarket chains from 2006 to 2019, in the working paper of 2021, p. 7
[strulovshlain2022]: "The final sample consists of 77 million
observations of 3461 products, in 1498 stores, with a total of 11.8
billion units sold for $43 billion." and "41 percent of prices end with
99, and 87 percent end with 9 as the last digit." Its Table 1, p. 34,
gives the share of 9-ending prices as 0.69 in the full data of 5,121
million observations before any cleaning and 0.87 after it, and the
share of 0-ending prices as 0.16 and 0.07; p. 16: "Further, there is a
noticeable spike at 49-ending prices with 14 percent of the total
prices." The cleaning keeps the chains and stores with few prices ending
in 1, 2 or 3, which the paper reads as averages of several prices rather
than prices paid, p. 51.

## Threats

- Selection. Eight works read in full out of the nine that passed the
  screening of 2026-09-30; the ninth, [bray2006], was excluded at full
  text because the 64 percent it carries is a newspaper's figure and not
  a distribution it reports. The nine came from the sixty most cited
  records of one index, and no later pass has searched beyond them.
- Representation. The works count different things, the cent digit of a
  scanner price, the rightmost printed digit of an advertised price, the
  rightmost nonzero digit of an integer price, and in the ten-country
  survey a digit chosen per country by where nonzero endings become
  significant, so the percentages are not one distribution and the claim
  rests on their common shape, a few digits carrying most endings.
- Age and place. The samples date from 1989 to 2019 and five of the
  eight are American; the survey of 2001 shows the leading digit moving
  with the market, and a currency without a cent coin or a market the
  samples miss may differ.
- Version. [strulovshlain2022] is quoted from its working paper of 2021,
  whose figures and pages may differ from the published article of 2022
  that the bibliography cites.
